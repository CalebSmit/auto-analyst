## Intent

Date a table's columns from the years the page prints above them, and refuse to date a table that has lost one. The two halves are one mechanism and each is dangerous alone: writing the years into a table's headings is what lets the period detector settle instead of escalating every comparative statement, and a table that dropped a column would then settle on a wrong date. So the same read that names the columns is the read that notices one is missing - non-consecutive years mean a column between them was never built. Report rather than repair: reconstructing the missing column is a guess, and saying its column indices cannot be trusted is a fact.

## Tasks

- [x] Date a table's columns from the nearest band above it that puts a bare year at every column, with the years distinct
- [x] Keep the heading a table already has: the new read fills in where there is nothing, and never overwrites
- [x] Report a table whose dated years skip as a table that has lost a column, and do not let it settle
- [x] Record why the wider rule was refused: a sentence crossing the columns satisfies it, and no share-of-words threshold separates the sentence from a real header
- [x] Calibrate over every multi-column table on both filings, and say what changed and what did not

## Exit criteria

*The phase closes when all of these are true and `gate close` says so.*

- A comparative statement in a PDF names its columns, and the period detector settles on it instead of reporting that no header row names them
- No table that names its columns today changes what it says, over every multi-column table on both filings
- The table that dropped a column is reported as having dropped one, and does not settle - proved on 3D Systems p59, where the filer's XBRL says the figures at column 2 are 2023's
- A sentence that happens to cross the columns dates nothing - proved on the two pages where the wider rule read a date out of prose
